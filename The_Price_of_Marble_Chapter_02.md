# THE PRICE OF MARBLE
### A Historical Novel

*Rome, AD 112*

---

# Chapter II — A Name in Wax

## Scene One

Marcus Vibius Priscus had inherited a house on the Palatine, a reputation older than the paving stones and a family that believed inheritance was the same thing as virtue. He stood in his father’s library while his sister Cornelia watched him choose his words as if they were pieces in a game. Morning light came through the shutters in narrow bars, catching the dust above the table. A bronze lamp still smelled faintly of last night’s oil. On the wall, a painted ancestor in a triumphal cloak regarded the living with the empty confidence of a man who had never had to ask whether he belonged.

“You invited her,” Cornelia said.

“I invited a useful person.”

“You invited Antonia Cassia.”

The name seemed to disturb the dust. Marcus laid down the letter he had been reading. “She knows men who speak carelessly.”

“And you believe she will speak carefully?”

“I believe she understands the cost of carelessness.”

Cornelia touched the edge of a bronze tablet, a household record kept by their mother. “You always did mistake silence for understanding.”

He turned toward the window. From there, Rome’s roofs looked orderly, each red tile in its place. The city beneath them was not. A report had arrived from the grain prefect’s office: discrepancies in public contracts, not large enough to alarm the emperor, but large enough to ruin a senator if attached to the right name. Marcus had found his name attached to one of them. Not as a beneficiary, the report said, but as the signatory to an old transfer that had enabled the current network of false accounts. The sum was enough to trouble his family and too little to attract the attention of men who saw only totals.

His father had taught him that government was mostly the art of deciding which inconveniences could be borne. He had also taught him that an inconvenience ignored long enough became a disaster belonging to someone else. Marcus had never quite determined whether this was wisdom or a confession.

“I am not asking her to save me,” he said.

“No. You never ask. You arrange the room so that refusal becomes expensive.”

“That is unfair.”

“Then stop arranging the room.”

A servant entered with a tray of bread, cheese and sliced pears. Cornelia waited until he left. She had been married twice, widowed once, and had raised her daughter in the Vibius house after her second husband died. Her manner had the steady economy of someone who had watched men call their preferences laws. She was younger than Marcus by three years and had spent most of her life repairing the consequences of decisions made by older men. Her authority in the house was informal, which meant she had to exercise it with care.

“You have not told me why her,” she said.

“Because she will hear about the missing cargo before I do.”

“Then why not simply ask?”

“Because if I ask, I place her in a position where she can refuse. If I invite her, at least I can learn whether she means to come.”

Cornelia’s eyebrows rose. “You understand the distinction and still defend it.”

“I am not defending it. I am explaining it.”

“Men explain a great deal when they wish to make an unkind choice sound inevitable.”

He bit into a pear and found it mealy. The taste reminded him of the old Vibius orchard at Aricia, where his father had sent him to learn the estate accounts. The first time Marcus had been trusted with the household’s books, he had been sixteen and pleased by the importance of his own handwriting. Antonia, not yet fifteen, had sat on the kitchen step with a broken stylus and corrected his sums. She had been a slave in his father’s household, though the word had seemed to him then like a legal category rather than a daily fact. She knew how much flour the kitchen used, how many lamps could be lit from a jar of oil and how much the steward stole when no one checked. Marcus had been astonished by her ability. He had never asked why a girl so able had no say over what she did with it.

“I thought the matter was settled,” Cornelia said, drawing him back.

“What matter?”

“Your guilt.”

“I am not guilty of anything.”

“That has never stopped you looking guilty.”

He set down the pear. “You think I invited her because of the past.”

“I think you invited her because you do not know how to speak of the past except by making it useful.”

“The invitation is about a public account.”

“And the old house ledger?”

Marcus’s gaze moved to the tablet under Cornelia’s fingers. It held the household records from the year their mother fell ill. His mother had kept the accounts herself until her hands began to tremble. After that, servants maintained them, but she insisted on seeing each tablet before it was stored. The ledger contained a page that had been cut away before the family archive moved from the old house. Marcus had never asked why. He had been too young to understand its significance and too old, afterward, to claim ignorance.

“How did you hear of that?” he asked.

“Mother told me.”

“She told you there was a missing page?”

“She told me there were some things your father wanted forgotten.”

“My father is dead.”

“The people he protected are not.”

A knock sounded at the library door. This time the servant was a young secretary, Naso, who carried a narrow wooden case. He wore a clean tunic and an expression of carefully managed anxiety. “A note from the grain office, sir.”

Marcus broke the seal. The note was a copy of a request for clarification: the office wanted confirmation of a transfer authorized eighteen years before, from the Vibius estate to an account held by an intermediary. The original sum was modest. The destination was not named. The signature was his.

He remembered the wax beneath his hand, the folded instruction from his father, the words *sign here* and *it is a temporary matter*. He remembered wanting to be trusted. He did not remember asking where the money had gone.

“Send a reply stating I will appear in person,” he said.

Naso hesitated. “The prefect’s office expects an answer by noon.”

“Then I will give it before noon.”

Cornelia folded her hands. “You will tell them what you know?”

“I will tell them what I remember.”

“That is a safer phrase.”

“It is also the truth.”

She released the tablet. “Truth can still be incomplete.”

He placed the note beside the invitation to Antonia. Two pieces of wax, each small enough to hold between finger and thumb, seemed to weigh more than the house around them. One bore the authority of the state. The other bore the name of a woman who had once trusted him.

## Scene Two

The grain prefect’s office stood beside the basilica, where the smell of damp paper competed with incense drifting from a nearby shrine. Marcus entered through a side door used by petitioners and clerks. He wore no retinue and no ceremonial toga, only a plain tunic beneath a toga praetexta whose purple edge announced his rank whether he wished it to or not. At the threshold, a doorkeeper bowed too deeply. The clerk inside offered a chair without looking at him. On the wall, a notice listed the hours for petitions and the penalties for obstructing an official. Someone had scratched a crude drawing beneath the last line. The clerk had tried to scrape it away, leaving a pale scar in the plaster. Marcus wondered how many corrections the city made that only made the original mark more visible.

“Senator,” said the prefect’s deputy, a narrow-faced man called Valerius. “We expected a written answer.”

“You received one.”

“We expected a more precise one.”

“You have my signature. I cannot make it more precise without seeing the record.”

Valerius opened a file box. “The transfer was issued from your family estate to an account used by a grain broker. It was recorded as repayment for a loan.”

“Which loan?”

“That is what the office would like to establish.”

“Then we agree on the question.”

Valerius did not smile. He unfolded a copy of the account. The sum had been entered in the year Marcus first began handling household affairs. The figure was not large enough to impoverish an estate, but it was large enough to finance a shipment or two. Beneath it appeared his signature, still youthful in its angular certainty.

“Did you issue this payment?” Valerius asked.

“I signed the transfer.”

“Did you know its purpose?”

“I was told it covered a debt.”

“Did you see the debt?”

“No.”

“Did you ask to see it?”

Marcus felt the question settle into him. The office was quiet; outside, a cart struck a rut and someone swore. “No.”

Valerius tapped the page. “This transfer predates the current discrepancies. It may be unrelated.”

“Then why ask me?”

“Because the name of the recipient appears again in recent shipping records.”

“Whose name?”

“Serranus.”

Marcus looked at the clerk. He had suspected the contractor’s involvement but not the connection to the old payment. “Aulus Serranus?”

“The same.”

“Was he the original recipient?”

“The record does not say. It lists an intermediary who later entered his service.”

The deputy closed the file box. “There are people who would prefer this be treated as a clerical error. The grain reached the city. No formal shortage was declared. The accounts can be corrected quietly.”

“Can they?”

Valerius’s mouth tightened. “Senator, I am not asking you to approve a false record. I am suggesting that the old transfer need not become an affair of state.”

“Then it should not trouble you if the record is examined.”

“It may trouble your family.”

“Which part?”

“Your name is attached to it.”

“My signature is.”

“That distinction may not survive a public hearing.”

Marcus recognized the warning. He had spent years in rooms where men delivered threats as if they were practical advice. He understood the usefulness of quiet. A senator who had served the empire for decades could survive an awkward question, but not every house within his protection could. The land at Aricia provided income for Cornelia, Julia and a handful of distant dependents whose names rarely appeared in conversation. If the inquiry widened, creditors might move against the estate. If it did not, the grain that had been promised to the city would remain a number in a book rather than bread in a hand.

“What do you recommend?” he asked.

“I recommend patience.”

“Patience is often the name given to waiting until someone else is too tired to insist.”

“Not always.”

“No. Sometimes it is wisdom.”

Valerius leaned back. “You are a careful man, Priscus.”

“I have tried to be.”

“Careful men do not need to volunteer for every difficulty.”

Marcus stood. “Then I shall try to be less careful.”

He asked for a copy of the ledger and was told it could not be released while the investigation continued. The deputy did permit him to inspect the entry again. Marcus stared at the old signature until it seemed to belong to another person. He had been young enough to assume that his father’s judgment was the world’s judgment. He had been old enough to know that a command could still be questioned. The shame was not that he had signed; it was that he had never considered the signature might make someone else pay.

In the corridor, he found Cornelia waiting. She had come to deliver a letter from Julia, who was studying with a tutor in the west wing of the house. The letter contained a drawing of a horse with a comically long face and a question about whether her mother thought she could delay a marriage negotiation without causing a quarrel. Cornelia folded it and placed it in her sleeve.

“You should not have come,” Marcus said.

“I should not have to.”

“What do you mean?”

“I mean your affairs are rarely private if they can affect the house.”

He told her about Serranus and the older transfer. Cornelia listened without interrupting. When he finished, she looked toward the basilica door, where petitioners had begun to gather.

“Mother kept the account because she knew your father had used you,” she said.

“He did not use me. He instructed me.”

“Is that distinction helping you?”

“I am not sure.”

“You have spent a lifetime making sure it does.”

They walked back across the Forum. A peddler sold fried dough from a covered tray. Two scribes argued over a public notice. A veteran with a stiff leg leaned against a column, watching the crowds pass as if the city were a parade that had forgotten to end. Marcus thought of the grain cargoes and the people who would pay for their absence. He had made decisions about such people through documents, as if a line on wax could not be carried home by someone’s empty hands.

At the edge of the square, Cornelia stopped. “If you ask Antonia for information, be honest about why.”

“I intend to be.”

“You intend to be accurate. That is not always the same.”

## Scene Three

The dinner at the Vibius house was set for the third evening after the invitation. Antonia’s carriage arrived at dusk, its wheels moving slowly over the Palatine paving. She had asked to be brought by her own driver and brought no visible escort. Marcus had been waiting in the atrium long enough for the servants to notice and not long enough to admit that he had been waiting.

He had rehearsed a greeting on the afternoon of her arrival. Every version sounded like a demand dressed in courtesy. At last he decided to say her name and let the silence do what it would.

“Cassia,” he said.

“Priscus.”

She wore a dark green stola with a plain clasp at the throat. The fabric was fine, but it did not announce its cost. A gold bracelet sat at her wrist, the same narrow style he remembered from years ago, though it could not be the same piece. Her hair was bound back with pins. She looked composed, neither inviting nor forbidding. He had once believed he could read every expression on her face. He had learned that confidence was not the same as understanding.

“My sister Cornelia,” he said.

“I know who she is.”

Cornelia inclined her head. “I have heard a great deal about you.”

“I hope not all from Marcus.”

“Not all.”

For a moment something like amusement passed between the women. Marcus felt himself outside the exchange, and the feeling was familiar enough to sting. He led them through the atrium and into the dining room. The painted panels showed a garden that did not exist, a place of shade and still water where no servant carried a dish and no guest had to remember what he owed.

There were only four places at the table: Marcus, Cornelia, Antonia and a legal adviser named Sabinus. The small number was meant to suggest confidence. It also meant every silence would be noticed. The meal began with eggs, olives and a little fish. Conversation stayed with public works, the weather and the price of oil. Antonia asked after the road to Aricia. Cornelia described a bridge repair that had taken longer than the prefect’s engineers had promised. Sabinus complained about the number of petitions arriving at his office. Marcus found himself listening to the rhythm of Antonia’s voice rather than to her words.

“Do you still keep the household accounts yourself?” Cornelia asked her.

“Some of them.”

“Why not all?”

“Because a house that depends on one person’s memory is a house already in trouble.”

Cornelia nodded. “That is good advice.”

“Advice is cheaper than labor.”

“Not if it is ignored.”

Antonia looked at Marcus. “You should tell your sister that more often.”

“I do.”

“No,” Cornelia said. “He tells me I am right after he has done what he wanted.”

Sabinus laughed. Marcus did not. He watched Antonia lift her cup. The gesture was ordinary, but he remembered her hands ink-stained in the old kitchen, fingers quick over the household tablets. He remembered the last evening he had seen her before leaving Rome: the door of his father’s office closing behind him, the manumission document on the table, the words he had meant to say and the ones he had chosen instead.

When the first course was removed, Marcus asked the servants to leave them. Cornelia and Sabinus exchanged a glance. Antonia did not. The room quieted. Outside, the household continued: footsteps in the corridor, a bowl set down, a door latched. The private dining room was not soundproof. It only encouraged people to pretend it was.

“I invited you because the grain office has found an old transfer bearing my signature,” Marcus said. “It may connect to the missing shipments.”

“Then you should have said so in the letter.”

“I did not know what I would find.”

“You knew enough to ask.”

“Yes.”

“Why bring me here rather than speak in my house?”

“Because the record belongs to my family.”

“Does the money?”

He paused. “I do not know.”

Antonia set down the cup. “That is the first useful answer you have given.”

Cornelia shifted in her chair, but did not interrupt. Marcus explained the transfer: the date, the sum, the signature, the explanation his father had given him. Antonia listened with the attention she gave a new account. She did not soften his language or supply an excuse. When he described the intermediary, she asked whether he remembered the man’s name. He did not. She asked whether his father had shown him the debt being repaid. He had not seen it. She asked whether he had asked.

“No,” he said again.

“Why?”

“I trusted my father.”

“Trust does not make a record accurate.”

“No.”

“Did you believe the transfer would harm anyone?”

“No.”

“Did you ask who might be harmed?”

“No.”

The questions were not shouted. They did not need to be. Marcus felt each one return him to the boy who had thought a signature was merely a way of proving he had done as he was told. He had spent much of his life trying to become a man who made his own decisions. He had not examined how many of them still depended on the same old assumptions.

“You want me to tell you what I have heard,” Antonia said. “I may do that. But I will not lend you my name to make the matter look cleaner.”

“I am not asking you to.”

“You are asking me to come to your house, hear your explanation, and decide whether you are useful to my interests. That is not the same thing as asking nothing.”

Cornelia’s eyes lowered. Sabinus studied the table. Marcus did not look away.

“What would you have me ask?” he said.

“Ask whether the grain reached the city. Ask who signed for it. Ask whether the men who moved it knew it was missing. Ask what happened to the people who depend on the distribution. Do not begin with what this will do to your family.”

“My family may be implicated.”

“Then your family may have to answer.”

The sentence seemed to quiet the room around them. Marcus had heard sharper accusations from men who wanted something. Antonia’s words were difficult because she did not seem to enjoy delivering them.

“What have you heard?” he asked.

“I have heard that a cargo was moved under a second manifest. I have heard the name Serranus. I have heard a clerk at the grain office has begun asking questions.”

“Nicanor,” Marcus said.

Her eyes did not change, but he saw that she registered his knowledge. “You know his name.”

“The office mentioned the clerk.”

“Then perhaps he is already in danger.”

“Has anyone threatened him?”

“I do not know.”

“You said he has been asking questions.”

“That is not an answer to your question.”

“No.”

Antonia glanced at Cornelia. “You should consider who else can see the ledgers. The clerk is not the only person who may understand the difference between what was unloaded and what was entered.”

Marcus leaned forward. “If you know where the records are—”

“I know where some records might be.”

“And you will help me find them?”

“I will help determine whether they exist. Do not mistake that for an alliance.”

“What would you call it?”

“A conversation.”

The meal resumed, though no one seemed hungry. Sabinus asked a question about the prefect’s jurisdiction, and Marcus answered. Cornelia spoke of Julia’s studies. Antonia listened, then asked about the young woman’s planned marriage. Cornelia’s expression changed by the smallest degree.

“It is under discussion,” she said.

“Is it Julia’s choice?” Antonia asked.

“It is the family’s arrangement.”

“That is not what I asked.”

Cornelia’s hand rested on the stem of her cup. “She has not yet been asked.”

“Then she has not yet agreed.”

“It is customary to arrange these things before asking.”

“Custom is very useful to people who already have the power to decide.”

Marcus moved to speak, but Cornelia answered first. “You are right.”

Antonia regarded her. “I did not expect you to say that.”

“Why not?”

“Because it is inconvenient.”

“Most truths are.”

They looked at each other, and Marcus saw a respect forming between them that he could neither claim nor interrupt. He remembered that Antonia had never wanted merely to be admitted into a room. She wanted to know who had built it, whose labor held it up and who had been told not to enter.

After supper, Cornelia took Sabinus to see the household archive. She said they needed to check the tax rolls before the morning. Marcus knew she was giving him a chance to speak privately with Antonia. He also knew she would expect him to use the chance well.

They stood in the atrium beside the impluvium. The water held the reflection of the ceiling in a small, broken square. Antonia looked down at it rather than at him.

“You could have written to me before,” she said.

“I could have.”

“You did not.”

“No.”

“Why?”

He could have said he had been busy, that he had feared reopening an old wound, that he had not known whether she wanted to hear from him. Each answer would have been true and insufficient. “I was ashamed,” he said.

“Of what?”

“Of having left you without an explanation.”

“You gave me an explanation.”

“I gave you a document.”

“You gave me a legal status. You did not give me a reason.”

“I thought the freedom mattered more.”

“It did. It mattered greatly. It did not answer the question.”

He searched for the words he should have spoken then. The years had not made them easier. “My father told me that if I delayed, he would sell you to another household. He told me the manumission was the only thing I could do for you without drawing attention to what had passed between us.”

“And you believed him.”

“Yes.”

“Did you think I would thank you?”

“I hoped you would be safe.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

“No.”

He thought she might leave. She remained, her gaze resting on the water. “I was safe enough to begin. That is more than many people get. I am not saying you did nothing for me. I am saying you did what you could bear to do and let me carry the rest.”

Marcus absorbed the words without argument. He had spent years understanding the past as a tragedy in which he had been powerless. The truth was harder and less elegant. He had possessed some power, and had used it in a way that saved one part of her life while abandoning another.

“I am sorry,” he said.

Antonia looked at him then. “That is a beginning.”

“Is it enough?”

“No.”

The answer was immediate, but not cruel. Outside, a watchman called the hour. The sound passed over the rooftops and faded.

“Will you help me find the records?” he asked.

“I will look. I will not promise what I cannot control.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

“I am trying.”

She accepted the distinction. At the outer door, she paused beneath the bronze lark. “You invited me because you thought I knew something you needed.”

“Yes.”

“You were right.”

“And now?”

“Now you know that I know it.”

“That is not the same as helping.”

“No. It is the beginning of a fairer conversation.”

She left without waiting for his reply. The carriage rolled down the hill. Marcus remained in the doorway until its sound disappeared into the city.

Cornelia returned from the archive. “How did it go?”

“She may help.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

She watched the empty doorway. “You told her about Father.”

“I told her what he said.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

“You sound like her.”

“I have had more years to consider him.” Cornelia crossed to the impluvium. “Mother kept the household records because she feared what he would leave behind. She did not tell me every detail. She told me enough to understand that he could make a kindness serve his own purpose.”

Marcus leaned against the doorframe. “Do you think he meant to protect her?”

“Perhaps. He also meant to protect himself. It is possible to do both.”

“He said the manumission would prevent a worse fate.”

“Was that true?”

“I believe so.”

“Then the act was good. The silence was not.”

He looked at the water, where the ceiling’s image trembled. “I thought she would understand.”

“You wanted her to understand without having to hear the explanation.”

“Yes.”

“That is a common wish.” Cornelia’s voice softened. “It is not a fair one.”

They had lived through the same household and remembered different fathers. Cornelia remembered a man who could be attentive when it served him, who sent gifts to a wife he rarely listened to, who praised his children when their actions reflected well on him. Marcus remembered the man who had taken him to the Senate and placed his hand on his shoulder before introducing him as the future of the family. Neither memory was false. Together they made a portrait less comfortable than either alone.

“Do you think I should have refused him?” Marcus asked.

“You were seventeen.”

“I was old enough to sign.”

“Old enough to sign is not old enough to understand the consequences. But you are old enough now.”

He heard no absolution in it. Cornelia was not offering one. She was returning responsibility to the person who could still act.

A servant announced that Julia had returned from her lessons. The girl entered carrying a writing tablet and a sprig of rosemary tucked behind her ear. She had heard the tail of the conversation and looked from one adult to another.

“Have I interrupted something?” she asked.

“Only your uncle learning to answer a question,” Cornelia said.

Julia gave Marcus a look of theatrical sympathy. “It must be difficult.”

“It is.”

She produced her tablet. On it were the lines of a Greek verse, copied twice and corrected once. Her tutor had written a note praising her attention and suggesting she continue with rhetoric. Cornelia read it, pleased, then began to speak of the marriage arrangement. Julia’s smile disappeared.

“Has my intended said anything?” she asked.

“Not yet,” Cornelia replied. “There will be another meeting.”

“Will I be there?”

“You will.”

“Will I be asked what I think?”

Cornelia paused. “You will have the chance to speak.”

“That is not the same as being asked.”

Marcus saw Antonia’s influence in the question, not as an instruction but as permission. Cornelia noticed it too. Her fingers tightened on the tablet, then relaxed.

“You are right,” she said. “I should ask you.”

Julia glanced between them. “Then ask.”

Cornelia drew a breath. “Do you want this marriage?”

“No.”

The answer was quiet and absolute. The room seemed to change around it. Cornelia’s eyes closed for a moment. Marcus watched his sister meet a truth she had suspected and hoped not to hear.

“Then we will discuss what can be done,” Cornelia said.

“Will it ruin us?” Julia asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Will it make you angry with me?”

“No.” Cornelia reached for her hand. “But I may be frightened. Do not mistake that for anger.”

Julia accepted the hand, though she did not lean into it. The exchange was not a resolution. It was simply the first honest conversation the household had allowed her to have about a decision everyone claimed was already settled.

Marcus looked toward the street. The night had deepened. He had invited Antonia to solve a problem and found, instead, that she had made him see the problems his household had hidden in plain sight. He wondered how many other rooms in the house were built over unexamined accounts.

The next morning, a messenger arrived from the grain office. Nicanor had not reported for work. His room had been found empty, his blanket folded, and the wooden box beneath his cot open. The clerk’s neighbors remembered two men asking for him the night before. One wore a ring with a boar beneath a laurel branch.

Marcus read the report and looked toward the window. The city was still asleep in some districts and already furious in others. He sent for his carriage, then stopped before the servant could fetch it. Antonia would know what to do. But the first question was not whether he could reach her. It was whether he could make himself useful before he asked her to carry another burden.

He ordered a discreet search for Nicanor and his family. He told the household secretary to preserve the old records. He wrote to the prefect that he would appear again and give a full account of the transfer. None of these actions could undo his old signature. They were only the next lines in a ledger that had been opened at last.
