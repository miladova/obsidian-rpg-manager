# THE PRICE OF MARBLE
### A Historical Novel

*Rome, AD 112*

---

# Chapter I — The House of the Lark

## Scene One

At dawn, when the city’s first carts groaned toward the markets and the bakery ovens breathed heat into the lanes, Antonia Cassia stood beneath the painted ceiling of her dining room and watched a hairline crack creep through the blue plaster. She had noticed it the night before, between the last guests and the servants sweeping away crushed figs. Now the crack ran from a painted swallow’s beak to the gold border. It was a small defect, no wider than a thread. Small defects were the most expensive kind. They invited people to imagine the rest of the house was failing.

“Call the plasterer,” she told Dama, her steward. “Not the one who charged us for the ceiling twice.”

Dama folded his hands into the broad sleeves of his tunic. “He says the first repair was a gift.”

“Then let him gift us another.”

His mouth shifted, almost a smile. The two of them had worked together long enough to understand when a joke was a command and when a command was a test. Dama had been a household slave in the years when Antonia was still learning which doors opened inward and which opened only for men. She had purchased his freedom after he helped her leave a house where neither freedom nor kindness had been offered. He had stayed because he wanted to, though neither of them pretended that wanting was the whole of the matter. The House of the Lark had given him a position, a room with a shutter that latched, and the right to say no when the answer mattered. Antonia had given him nothing he had not earned.

Below them the house woke in layers: the clink of jars in the kitchen, the rasp of brooms on mosaic, the slatted shutters opening to the narrow courtyard. A fountain whispered over its basin. Somewhere in the lane a donkey brayed with the outrage of a consul. Through an upper window came the soft notes of a lyre, then a stop, then the same phrase again. One of the musicians was practicing before the guests could hear the mistakes.

The House of the Lark occupied a narrow plot between a perfumer’s shop and a money-changer’s counting room. Its outer wall was plain, deliberately so. The door was painted red, with a small bronze bird above the lintel. Inside, a visitor passed through an atrium where water gathered in a shallow impluvium and sunlight fell in a square. Past it lay dining rooms, bedrooms, a garden, a bath chamber, and the private rooms in which Antonia kept the accounts. The city knew what the house was. The city also knew what it was permitted to call it. Antonia had learned that names were a kind of property: the wealthy could afford to lend them; the poor had them taken away.

Men came to the House of the Lark for supper, music, conversation, beautiful company and the relief of being among people who did not ask them to perform the parts they played in public. Some arrived with honest appetites. Others brought petitions, bargains, threats or secrets disguised as all three. Antonia welcomed each in the same measured tone. Her door was open to those who paid; her mind was not.

A courier waited in the atrium, rain-dark cloak folded over one arm. The city had not seen rain for four days, so Antonia guessed the damp came from the river rather than the sky. He carried a letter sealed with dark red wax. The impression showed a laurel branch crossed with a spear: the emblem of the Vibius household.

Dama glanced at her, saw her stillness, and dismissed the servants with a tilt of his head. The courier bowed. “From Senator Marcus Vibius Priscus.”

The name had not been spoken inside her house for fourteen years. Antonia accepted the letter but did not break the seal until the courier had gone. The wax cooled against her thumb. Its crisp edge pressed a half-moon into the skin.

“Do you want me to burn it?” Dama asked.

“I want to know what it says.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.” She turned the letter over. “It is usually more dangerous.”

She broke the seal. The message was short and written in a hand she recognized, though it had grown more formal. Marcus invited her to a private dinner at his house three evenings hence. He wished to consult her on a matter of common concern. Beneath the invitation he had added a line in his own hand: *I hope you will accept.*

Antonia let the page fall to the table. Hope, she thought, was a word men used when they wanted an answer but did not wish to name what refusal would cost.

“Send word,” she said. “I will come.”

Dama watched her. “You dislike his house.”

“I dislike many houses. I still know how to enter them.”

“Shall I arrange the carriage?”

“Yes. And send a message to Nicanor by the river. Ask whether the *Fortuna* has unloaded.”

Dama’s gaze sharpened. “You think the invitation is about the cargo.”

“I think a man with Marcus’s standing does not ask me to dinner because he has discovered a new appreciation for my company.”

“You might be surprised.”

“I have been surprised before. It taught me to count the silver afterward.”

She moved toward the door, then paused beneath the painted swallow. The crack crossed its beak now, a dark stroke against blue. It made the bird look as though it had opened its mouth to speak.

“Have the plasterer come today,” she said. “And tell him to preserve the swallow.”

The door to the dining room opened onto the small garden, but the room had not always belonged to her. When Antonia first saw the house, it had been a dyer’s workshop with a leaking roof and a courtyard choked with broken jars. The former owner had died owing money to a creditor who did not want the property; he wanted the rent from it. Antonia had wanted the property itself. She had a sum saved from years of managing other people’s households, a loan from a merchant who liked her arithmetic, and no family name to make the transaction respectable.

The seller had asked whether she meant to live there. Antonia had answered that she meant to make it useful. The answer had amused him. He had named a price high enough to test whether her ambition was a performance. She had named a lower one, itemizing the roof, the clogged drain, the cracked cistern and the cost of replacing the workshop’s warped doors. The seller laughed. Antonia had stood, thanked him for his time, and walked to the street. He called her back before she reached the corner.

She paid the deposit in coin, the balance in installments, and spent the next year learning that owning a building did not mean controlling all the forces that entered it. Rain found holes she had not seen. A neighbor complained about the noise. A tenant disappeared with a month’s rent. The first cook burned a pot large enough to feed twelve. The first musician brought a brother who stole a silver spoon and insisted the spoon had been a gift. Antonia dismissed them both, then hired the musician again three months later when she learned that his brother had gone to Ostia and would not return.

The house had grown by increments rather than miracle. The garden wall was repaired with stone bought from a demolished shop. The bath chamber came later, when she could afford a new water line and the labor to install it. The painted swallows were the idea of an artisan who had worked for one of the great families and wanted a commission of his own. He had offered her a procession of gods. She asked for ordinary birds.

“Gods have patrons,” she had told him. “Birds only need somewhere to land.”

The artisan had smiled, then painted a flock that appeared to be crossing the room in a single uncertain direction. Antonia had paid him two coins more than their agreement. Not because he had asked, but because the work deserved it. She had learned that being cheated was not the only way to lose money; sometimes refusing to recognize good work cost more.

Dama had come to the house during its second year. He was still legally enslaved then, assigned to a household whose master had begun selling off servants to cover debts. He had been sent to negotiate the sale of several wine jars. Antonia had noticed his careful attention to the room: the places he could see the door, the men whose voices changed when a woman spoke, the ledger tucked beneath her chair. She had asked him to calculate the cost of the wine in the jars. He had done it in his head, correcting the seller’s figures without making a show of it.

“Would you rather keep accounts than carry jars?” she had asked.

“I would rather choose what I do.”

“That is not one of the choices I can offer today.”

“No,” he had said. “It is not.”

She had been struck by his refusal to flatter her. Later, when his owner tried to sell him to a household in Campania, Antonia bought his contract. It was a transaction, not rescue. She had made certain he understood that before she signed. She would free him after he worked off the cost, she said, unless he preferred another arrangement. Dama had asked to see the account. They had argued about the price of his labor, the price of his food, the value of the work he had already done. When the balance was paid, he chose to stay. They had never again pretended that the choice was simple.

The House of the Lark began as three rooms and a kitchen. Its first guests were merchants too newly rich to be welcomed in the homes of senators, and old families too bored to admit they wanted company. Antonia learned who preferred their wine warmed, who had a habit of lying about their age, who would pay promptly and who expected the privilege of being reminded. She kept a book of debts, a book of wages, and a private book of names. The last was not a list of scandals. It was a record of who had once been kind when no advantage could be gained from it.

The house had made her wealthy by degrees. Wealth was less a pile of gold than the ability to say no without immediately wondering where she would sleep. It was the freedom to pay a physician before a fever became a funeral. It was the right to repair a roof before rain made a decision for her. She had more than that now: a carriage, a household, a reputation whispered in the dining rooms of men who would not publicly invite her to theirs. Still, the first measure of wealth remained the one she trusted. Could she refuse and remain standing?

## Scene Two

Only then did the day’s first guest arrive. Lucius Celer, a grain contractor whose new boots had never known the floor of a warehouse, was shown into the small reception room. He wore a gold ring heavy enough to bruise a hand and a tunic cut in the newest fashion. His hair was oiled, his beard closely trimmed, his expression arranged to suggest that the world had been glad to see him all his life. He wore a gold ring heavy enough to bruise a hand and a tunic cut in the newest fashion. His hair was oiled, his beard closely trimmed, his expression arranged to suggest that the world had been glad to see him all his life.

Antonia received him without rising from her chair. She wore a plain rust-colored stola with a bronze clasp at the shoulder. A narrow band of gold rested at her wrist. The bracelet had been her first luxury after the House began to prosper. She wore it when she wished people to remember that she had once possessed nothing and had not been ashamed of the fact.

“Your house grows more beautiful,” Celer said.

“It grows more expensive.”

“That, too, is a kind of beauty.”

“It is a kind of invoice.”

He laughed, uncertain whether she had made a joke at his expense. Celer was a man who needed the sound of laughter to tell him where he stood. He had contracts to supply grain for the public distribution, a warehouse at Ostia, and a chain of creditors he called partners. He spoke of weather, then tariffs, then the new works near the Forum. He praised the emperor’s foresight and the Senate’s diligence, though neither institution had ever invited him to sit at its table.

At last he came to the cargo. One shipment had been delayed, he said. A vessel called *Fortuna* had reached Ostia late and discharged only part of its load. He wanted to know whether Antonia had heard anything about the matter.

“Why would I?”

“Because you hear things.”

“I hear words. It is not the same as knowing what they mean.”

Celer leaned back. “There is a clerk at the grain office—Nicanor. A careful fellow, or so people say. He has begun asking questions about the manifests.”

“Perhaps he has discovered that a clerk’s work is to read them.”

“Questions can be troublesome.”

“Not if the answers are correct.”

His pleasant manner thinned. “You understand how commerce works, Antonia. A ship is delayed. A clerk is paid to smooth a discrepancy. The state receives grain. The city is fed. Everyone is content.”

“Everyone?”

“Everyone who matters.”

Antonia took up a small cup of watered wine. The afternoon had not yet warmed, but Celer’s confidence had already made the room feel close. “Then you have no cause to worry about questions.”

“I worry for men who may not understand how little they know.”

“You should worry for the men who understand and proceed anyway.”

Celer’s eyes narrowed. He had expected a favor or a price. Antonia gave him neither. When he stood to leave, he placed a small pouch on the table. It made a soft metallic sound.

“For the house,” he said.

“Your house or mine?”

“Yours, naturally.”

She did not touch it. “Dama will send it back.”

“Then I shall be insulted.”

“Only if you choose to be.”

Celer left with his purse and his smile, though the latter sat less securely than when he had arrived. After the door closed, Dama entered from the corridor.

“Shall I send for Nicanor?”

“Not yet. Ask the dockside bookkeeper about the *Fortuna*. No names, no promises. Find out what was unloaded and who signed for it.”

“And if the bookkeeper wants payment?”

“Pay him for his time. Not his silence.”

Dama nodded. “The distinction may be difficult for him.”

“Then make it plain.”

He left to give the order. Antonia stood at the open window and looked down into the lane. A fishmonger was arguing with a woman who had found a scale still clinging to her purchase. A child carried a stack of tiles on his back. Two clients in white togas waited beneath a portico for a patron who had not yet appeared. The city was an orchestra tuning badly: every instrument noisy, none willing to stop.

When Antonia had first come to Rome, she had owned a single tunic and a pair of sandals tied with cord. She had slept in a loft above a kitchen where rats moved through the thatch with more confidence than the humans below. The woman who had brought her there told her that a girl with no relations could be made useful, or could be made invisible. Antonia had chosen useful. She learned to add, to read a household ledger, to recognize the difference between a debt and an excuse. She learned which doors to open, which to bar, and how to look at a man until he remembered that she was also looking back.

The first years had not been a story of triumph. They had been years of bargains made with too little information. She had trusted people who were kind because they were kind, and learned too late that kindness could be a tool. She had been protected, then kept. She had been praised for cleverness so long as it served someone else. When she finally escaped the household that held her, it was not because a rescuer arrived. It was because she had saved enough, recognized the right moment, and persuaded a guard that the trouble of stopping her would cost more than letting her pass.

Freedom had not arrived as a door thrown wide. It had arrived as a document, a small sum, a borrowed cloak and a road on which every traveler had a reason to look away. Her first room in Rome had leaked. Her first business had failed. Her second had succeeded because she stopped believing that men who called themselves honorable would behave honorably without a reason.

The House of the Lark was the third thing she built. She had chosen its name for a bird she once saw perched on the rim of a well: an ordinary creature singing above a place where people came to draw water. She liked the thought that something could rise from the common necessities of the city and be heard.

By midday, the dockside bookkeeper had sent a message. The *Fortuna* had arrived ten days before the date listed on Celer’s account. It had discharged grain, but not enough to match its manifest. The remaining sacks had been logged under a second vessel’s mark, then moved inland. A man named Serranus had signed for the transfer.

Antonia read the wax tablet twice. Aulus Serranus was a contractor with political friends, a sharp appetite for profit and a habit of confusing intimidation with authority. She had met him at dinners where he spoke of public order while servants replaced the silver he had thrown. His family’s name had been respectable for three generations. His own manners suggested he had mistaken respectability for permission.

“Is there proof?” Dama asked.

“There is a mark in a book.”

“Enough to act?”

“Enough to be careful.”

She returned the tablet to its case. “Send word to Nicanor. Tell him I want to see him. No messenger from this house should carry the message directly. Use the spice seller.”

Dama gave her a level look. “A careful arrangement.”

“A careful arrangement is cheaper than a funeral.”

## Scene Three

At noon, Antonia found Livia in the courtyard, rehearsing a song for the evening. Livia had come to the House with a voice, a debt and a brother who gambled. She had repaid the first debt in installments, was negotiating the second, and had nearly forgiven the third. She wore a saffron veil tossed loosely over one shoulder and tapped the beat against the rim of the fountain with two fingers.

Her voice carried cleanly through the rooms. She could make a line about a sailor’s bad luck sound like a promise, and a line about a god’s favor sound like mockery. Guests listened because they liked the music. They returned because they wanted to believe she sang for them.

She stopped when Antonia entered. “You look as if you have swallowed a pin.”

“An old one.”

“Do you want it removed?”

“Not by anyone who charges by the hour.”

Livia set down the lyre. “Who sent the letter?”

“Marcus Vibius Priscus.”

The younger woman’s expression changed. She had heard enough of the name to know it mattered, though Antonia had never told her why. “The senator?”

“The same.”

“Will you go?”

“I have already said yes.”

“Then you have already decided.”

“That is not the same as wanting to go.”

Livia leaned her hip against the fountain. “You could send a refusal.”

“I could.”

“You could say you are ill.”

“I could.”

“You could send a letter that says the invitation was too late, too vague, too expensive, or too much trouble.”

“I could.”

“Do you hear how many choices you have?”

“I hear how many excuses you have found for me.”

Livia smiled. “I am practicing. The guests like a woman who can make a refusal sound like an invitation to ask again.”

Antonia looked toward the north gallery, where the rooms were being prepared for the evening. “That is not a skill I want you to need.”

“It is still a skill.”

“Yes.” Antonia sat on the low stone bench by the pool. “Keep it. But do not confuse being good at survival with having chosen the danger.”

Livia’s smile faded. “Sabina asked me this morning whether her uncle could take her home.”

“Has she spoken to him?”

“He has not come. She thinks he promised to.”

“Does she want to leave?”

“She says she does. Then she says she does not know what the house will charge her for the clothes and physician.”

“Has anyone shown her the account?”

“Not that I know.”

“Then bring it to her. Every line. No softened words.”

Livia considered this. “She may see the sum and decide to stay.”

“She may. Or she may see the sum and decide to go anyway. Both answers belong to her.”

“You know the house earns money from what she does here.”

“I know.”

“And the people who come here do not always know the difference between a woman who wants their company and one who needs the fee.”

“I know that, too.”

“Then sometimes the line is only a mark in the account.”

Antonia watched a single orange petal float along the fountain’s edge. The House offered women better terms than many places in the city. It offered them warmth, a doctor when they were ill, a choice among their clients when it could, and enough coin to create a path away. It also relied on the same world that made such choices narrow. A woman who came to Antonia’s house might be safer than she had been elsewhere and still not be safe. The fact that Antonia had made rules did not mean she had escaped the system that made rules necessary.

“Then we must not lie about the line,” Antonia said. “We can say where it is. We can say who drew it. We can move it when we have the power. But if we pretend it is not there, it will only cut deeper.”

Livia looked down at the lyre in her hands. “You make every answer sound like work.”

“It usually is.”

The house filled after sunset. Lamps were lit. The dinner room smelled of roasted fish, pepper, honey and warm bread. A merchant from Massilia arrived with an accountant. A magistrate brought a cousin whose toga had been brushed until it shone. Two men who claimed not to know each other spent the first hour pretending they had not noticed one another’s arrival. Livia sang, and the room quieted. Antonia moved among the guests, present without lingering, listening without seeming to.

A courier arrived while the first course was being served. This one wore no house colors and carried no visible seal. Dama intercepted him at the entrance and brought him to Antonia in the passage outside the dining room. The man’s hands were clean but his sandals were worn. He was either careful or new to the work.

“Message from the spice seller,” he said.

Antonia took the small wax tablet. Nicanor would meet at the tally house above the river after first light. He had asked that she come without a retinue.

“Did he write this himself?” she asked.

The courier nodded. “He used the shopkeeper’s wax.”

“Did anyone follow you?”

“I did not look behind me.”

“That is not the same as no.”

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

She appreciated the answer. She paid him, then sent Dama to arrange for two guards to wait at a distance. She would go to the meeting with no obvious escort and no expectation of safety.

By the time the guests had left, the lamps were low and the kitchen had been swept. The crack in the ceiling remained, dark against the blue plaster. The plasterer had not come. Antonia stood beneath it with a lamp raised, trying to see how far it ran. It vanished into the shadow above the gold border.

The letter from Marcus lay in the private room beside the unfinished account books. She had thought she would burn it once the dinner was arranged. Instead, she had folded it and placed it among the day’s papers. She did not know whether she wanted to preserve the invitation or merely keep it where she could see it.

Livia found her there, out of costume, her hair loosened from its pins. “You are still looking at the ceiling.”

“It is getting worse.”

“Perhaps the plasterer will come tomorrow.”

“Perhaps.”

“You should sleep.”

“I have a meeting at dawn.”

“Then you should sleep before dawn.”

Antonia laughed once. “You have become intolerably practical.”

“I learned from the best.”

Livia went to her room. Antonia remained. The house settled around her: a servant’s cough in the corridor, a latch clicking, water shifting in the fountain. Beyond the walls, Rome was not quiet. A cart rattled over stone. Someone was arguing in the lane. A watchman called the hour. The city’s noise had been part of her life for so long she only noticed its absence in places where she could not hear it.

She unfolded Marcus’s letter once more. *I hope you will accept.* In the hand of another man, it might have been a courtesy. In his, it was a question carrying the weight of years. She wondered whether he still remembered the girl she had been, the one who believed that a promise became true if both people wanted it to. She wondered whether she remembered him accurately, or whether time had turned him into a villain because the alternative—that he had loved her and still failed her—was harder to bear.

Antonia set the letter down. She had a ledger to read, a clerk to meet, and a house full of people who depended on her judgment. The past could wait until it learned how to pay rent.

Just before sleep, she heard a soft knock at the outer door. Dama answered. A boy’s voice whispered from the street. There was no message, only a warning: two men had been asking after Nicanor near the river. The boy did not know their names. He knew that one carried a knife and the other a ring showing a boar beneath a laurel branch.

Dama came to her room while the boy waited in the atrium, his damp hair dripping onto the mosaic. He had spoken fast, as if speed could protect him from what he had seen. Antonia asked him to repeat the warning slowly. The boy’s name was Gaius, though he answered only to Gai. He slept in the back of his aunt’s bread stall and ran errands for anyone who paid a copper. He had seen the two men outside the tally house, then again near the river stairs. The man with the knife had a split in one eyebrow. The man with the ring had a pale scar crossing the knuckle below it.

“Did they see you?” Antonia asked.

“I don’t think so.”

“Did they speak to you?”

“No.”

“Did you follow them?”

“I followed the one with the ring.”

“Why?”

Gai’s eyes moved toward the bread on the table. “I thought he might lead me to where they took Nicanor.”

“You thought it would be useful.”

He nodded, bracing for a scolding.

“Useful is not the same as safe.” She broke a loaf and handed him half. “Eat. Then tell Dama the route you took. He will make sure you can get back to your aunt without walking past them.”

The boy took the bread in both hands. “Will you pay me?”

“I will pay you for the warning. I will not pay you to follow armed men.”

“I didn’t know they were armed.”

“You knew enough to be afraid.”

He considered that, then ate. Dama took him to the kitchen and returned a little later with the route. The men had gone east. They had not entered the tally house, but they had waited outside long enough to make their interest plain. It was possible they had been sent to frighten Nicanor. It was possible they had been sent to find him. Either way, Antonia could no longer assume the clerk would reach the meeting alive.

Dama set a small knife on the table, hilt toward her. She did not pick it up. “Have the guards meet us at the eastern gate. Tell them we are changing the plan. No one goes to the river alone, and no one says Nicanor’s name in the street.”

“Should I wake Livia?”

“No. She has no reason to be part of this.”

“She will say that is not for you to decide.”

“She would be right.” Antonia paused. “Wake her, then. Tell her only that she may choose to stay here or go with you to the watch station. If she chooses to stay, she stays behind a barred door.”

Dama gave a short nod. There was no warmth in the orders, but there was no pretense that she could protect everyone by keeping them ignorant. Antonia had spent too many years watching people make decisions for her in the name of her own safety. She had no intention of repeating that mistake simply because she held the keys now.

The boy’s warning had turned the night into a ledger of risks. Nicanor could be hidden, but hiding him might endanger his sister. The men could be followed, but following them could reveal who was watching. Antonia could seek the city watch, but a watchman might owe Serranus a favor. Every answer opened a new account. She had learned that the worst decisions were made by people who believed there was a clean choice waiting somewhere just beyond the next door.

Antonia rose and dressed again. The night’s cool air met her at the stair. Somewhere above the rooftops, a lark called once, prematurely, and fell silent.

“Wake the guards,” she told Dama. “And send another message to the spice seller. Nicanor does not go to the tally house.”

“Where will you meet him?”

“I will decide after I learn whether he is still alive.”

The city had not yet begun to brighten. Antonia crossed the atrium, and the house opened around her, familiar and suddenly full of doors she would have to choose between.
